# 1. Story Identity

**Genre:** Cinematic intelligence thriller / story-driven RPG  
**Setting:** A fictionalized Southeast Asia, with A.E.G.I.S. headquarters in Manila, Philippines  
**Player role:** An ordinary person gradually recruited and trained as an intelligence operative  
**Narrative perspective:** First-person  
**Tone:** Mysterious, grounded, tense, character-driven, technologically sophisticated, and occasionally intimate or humorous through character interaction

Cyber Protocol is an RPG about becoming someone others can rely on when information is incomplete, truth is contested, and every decision has consequences.

The player does not grow by defeating monsters or collecting stronger weapons. They grow by learning to observe, question, investigate, understand systems, work with people, and make responsible decisions under pressure.

Programming is not a decorative minigame. It is one of the tools the player learns to use inside the story. Every technical challenge must have a narrative reason.

# 2. The Central Premise

An ordinary person opens Campaign and sees a black screen. An ordinary-looking in-game **Pesan Aman** notification appears. Tapping it opens a conversation with the official **A.E.G.I.S. / Recruitment Office** account—not Freya and not an unknown sender. The formal message explains that someone has given A.E.G.I.S. an order to recruit the player. The identity of the person behind that order is withheld.

The official invitation contains an in-game link. Tapping it opens the approved orientation and mission sequence. When the player enters the first mission, a second notification arrives from **Freya Knox**. Tapping it opens Freya's conversation, where she introduces herself as the player's mentor and offers to guide the missions. She does not say that she was the person who initiated the recruitment. Freya later provides a separate link to the **NEXUS** system; the NEXUS contact is not visible in the inbox until the player opens that link.

**Author-only continuity:** Freya is the person who initiated the recruitment order. This is a hidden story fact, not information to disclose in the official invitation or Freya's introduction. Preserve the mystery until a later, deliberately authored reveal.

Both notifications and all links are simulated inside the game. There is no real Android notification or external messaging service, and no separate "Unknown Sender" prologue. The central question is not merely who sent the invitation, but why Freya chose this person and what she knows that she has not yet shared.

As the player learns, investigates, and earns access, small discrepancies begin to form a larger picture. The most important discovery is not simply who changed a record or concealed a transaction. It is that someone may be shaping what different people and institutions are able to see.

The story should preserve uncertainty. Evidence can be confirmed, inferred, incomplete, or deliberately misleading. The player must learn to distinguish those states rather than accept every briefing as absolute truth.

# 3. The Narrative Promise

The player should experience five connected forms of progression:

1. **Competence:** learn programming and technical concepts, then apply them to meaningful tasks.
2. **Trust:** earn the confidence of the mentor, specialists, and A.E.G.I.S. divisions.
3. **Belonging:** move from an outsider to a recognized member of a team.
4. **Judgment:** make decisions with incomplete information and live with their consequences.
5. **Understanding:** gradually discover how separate cases connect to the campaign's larger mystery.

No single character or institution should possess the entire truth. Different agents have different responsibilities, access, assumptions, and blind spots.

# 4. Main Story Mystery — The Pattern Behind the Cases

## 4.1 The visible incidents

The first cases appear to be ordinary intelligence problems:
- A financial record that no longer matches its source documents.
- A communications route that appears inactive but leaves unexplained traces.
- A set of records whose timestamps and relationships do not agree.
- A technical system whose outputs are plausible individually but contradictory when compared.
- A report that has been repeated so often that its original source is difficult to identify.

These incidents are fictional and should be built from invented people, institutions, companies, locations, and datasets.

## 4.2 The hidden connection

**Proposed overarching mystery:** Someone is exploiting the gaps between systems, organizations, and human assumptions. Instead of simply stealing information, this hidden actor manipulates the integrity and interpretation of information—making false conclusions appear reasonable and genuine evidence appear unreliable.

The player gradually discovers recurring technical fingerprints and narrative clues across cases. These clues suggest coordination, but do not immediately prove who is responsible or why.

The mystery should not be solved by one magical database, one omniscient AI, or one villain explaining everything. It should emerge through evidence comparison, careful reasoning, character testimony, and the player's own work.

## 4.3 The opposing force

The primary opposing force remains deliberately unidentified during the opening campaign. It may involve a network of people with different motives rather than a single theatrical villain.

Do not reveal a definitive leader, organization name, complete motive, or full mechanism in the opening chapter. Introduce the opposition through consequences, traces, conflicting accounts, and indirect pressure.

# 5. Campaign Saga — Proposed Long-Range Structure

The following is a flexible narrative roadmap. It is not a promise that every campaign or chapter will ship in the first release.

## Campaign 01 — THE RECRUITMENT

**Primary theme:** Trust must be earned.  
**Player state:** Ordinary person → Recruit.  
**Narrative question:** Why did A.E.G.I.S. contact the player, and what is hidden inside the first case?

The player opens the official A.E.G.I.S. recruitment invitation, follows its mission link, receives Freya's separate introduction notification during the first mission, and gradually gains access to NEXUS through Freya's link. The player completes guided technical tasks and investigates a small discrepancy between two fictional system records. The discrepancy remains unresolved and becomes the first thread of a wider mystery.

The campaign ends with the player formally recruited and entrusted with a limited piece of information that the organization cannot yet explain.

## Campaign 02 — THE SILENT NETWORK

**Primary theme:** Connections are not always visible.  
**Player state:** Recruit / Initiate progressing toward an operational analyst role.  
**Narrative question:** Are the anomalies independent, or are they being coordinated?

The player works with specialists across Intelligence, Investigation, and Cyber Operations. Separate records, timelines, and fictional infrastructure traces begin to overlap. The player must compare sources, identify relationships, and challenge assumptions—including assumptions made inside A.E.G.I.S.

The campaign expands the cast and introduces disagreement about how far the organization should go to understand the pattern.

## Campaign 03 — THE GLASS ARCHIVE

**Primary theme:** Information can protect people, but it can also control them.  
**Player state:** Experienced operative with greater access and responsibility.  
**Narrative question:** Who controls the record of what happened—and who benefits when the record is believed?

The player reaches evidence that reframes earlier incidents. Some trusted accounts prove incomplete; some previously dismissed details become important. The player must decide how to handle sensitive discoveries, whom to involve, and what should be disclosed through authorized channels.

The campaign should deliver meaningful revelations while preserving consequences for earlier choices.

## Future campaign space

Later campaigns may explore internal A.E.G.I.S. tensions, international operations, the cost of institutional secrecy, and the player's changing responsibilities. These are narrative possibilities, not approved release commitments.

# 6. Campaign 01 — THE RECRUITMENT

## 6.1 Chapter 001 — FIRST CONTACT

**Purpose:** Turn the player from a curious outsider into a recruit who has a personal reason to continue.

The opening must be intimate before it becomes large-scale. Begin with the player's ordinary device and an unfamiliar message—not a headquarters tour, a lecture, or an action sequence.

### Opening sequence

**Opening cinematic — Official recruitment notification**

The first time the player opens Campaign, the screen becomes completely black. After a deliberate pause, an understated, ordinary-looking **Pesan Aman** notification appears. The sender is identified as **A.E.G.I.S. / Recruitment Office**. It must resemble a normal phone notification rather than a dramatic game mission banner.

The player must explicitly tap the notification. Tapping opens the in-game Secure Messenger directly to the official A.E.G.I.S. recruitment conversation. It must not open Freya's conversation, the generic inbox, or a separate unknown-sender prologue.

**Beat 1 — The official invitation**

The A.E.G.I.S. Recruitment Office sends a formal message. The approved core wording is:

> "Seseorang telah memberikan perintah kepada A.E.G.I.S. untuk merekrutmu."

The message explains that a person has issued the recruitment order, but the person's identity is withheld. It gives a concise introduction to A.E.G.I.S. and includes an in-game mission link. The player must tap **BUKA RANGKAIAN MISI** to enter the orientation sequence. The link must be an actual interactive game action, not decorative text.

The invitation is a fixed authored message from the organization. Do not attribute it to Freya. The official account remains visible in the Messenger inbox after it has been opened.

**Beat 2 — The orientation and first assignment**

The mission link opens the First Contact sequence. The player receives a limited A.E.G.I.S. orientation and a classified recruitment dossier, then begins a simple Python task using a variable and `print()`. The first task is designed for a beginner and does not assume prior programming experience.

**Beat 3 — Freya's separate notification**

When the first mission becomes active, a second in-game Pesan Aman notification appears over the mission screen. Its sender is **Freya Knox**. The player must tap it to open Freya's conversation. The message is a personal greeting: Freya introduces herself as the mentor who will accompany the player through the missions assigned by A.E.G.I.S.

Freya explains the immediate task, offers encouragement, and makes it clear that the player can ask questions. She does not disclose that she was the person who initiated the recruitment order, and the dialogue must not make that connection obvious.

The notification tap and the introduction state are persisted. If the app is interrupted before the player taps the notification, the same notification can be presented again when the first mission resumes. Once opened, it must not appear again unexpectedly.

**Beat 4 — The NEXUS contact link**

During the Freya conversation, she introduces NEXUS as an internal A.E.G.I.S. system for communication and technical support. Freya provides an interactive **NEXUS / KANAL SISTEM** link. The player must tap it to unlock and open the NEXUS conversation.

Before that action, NEXUS must not appear as a contact in the inbox, and its name must not be used as the player's apparent mentor. NEXUS is a system, not a human character. Freya remains the player's primary human mentor.

**Beat 5 — Official recruitment**

After the two introductory tasks are verified, A.E.G.I.S. offers the player Recruit status and limited access. This is a progression milestone, not the end of the campaign.

**Beat 6 — The first discrepancy**

A routine review reveals two receipt records for one training transmission. Their contents match, but their recorded times differ by a few seconds. The discrepancy is initially UNVERIFIED. The player is instructed to inspect the source data rather than assume tampering.

**Beat 7 — Audit Jejak Sistem**

The player receives a fictional event log containing information, warnings, and errors. A Python `for` loop and `if` condition are used to count only entries containing `ERROR`. The mission teaches iteration and conditional filtering while preserving the source log.

**Beat 8 — Inventaris Rekaman**

A second system provides a list of record references. The player uses `len()` to count the available items and records the result. The count is an inventory fact, not proof that every record is valid or complete.

**Beat 9 — Laporan Awal**

The player submits a short initial report that separates observations from interpretations. The audit count and record inventory are recorded with their sources. The time discrepancy remains unresolved; the player must not claim manipulation without evidence.

The opening sequence is an in-game cinematic simulation only. It must not request Android notification permission, create real system notifications, or access real messages or contacts. Both the A.E.G.I.S. invitation and Freya's notification are shown once per relevant checkpoint, with state recovery that prevents duplicate messages or silently skipping a required tap.

## 6.2 First technical mystery — A message with two layers

The first transmission contains two layers of meaning.

- The first layer is a guided beginner exercise. The player learns to store a simple piece of text in a variable and display it with `print()`.
- The second layer is a small follow-up challenge that asks the player to apply the just-learned idea to reveal another piece of the message.

The fictional transformation must be transparent and beginner-appropriate. Do not imply that a variable or simple text transformation is real encryption.

The reward is a story revelation: enough to establish that the contact is genuine or at least connected to a real organization, but not enough to explain why the player was selected.

## 6.3 First Contact follow-up — The Receipt Discrepancy

This follow-up remains within the approved First Contact training case. It does not introduce a separate financial case, a new organization, or an unapproved antagonist.

Two fictional A.E.G.I.S. training systems record receipt metadata for the same transmission. The payload appears identical, but the timestamps differ by a few seconds. The player is given two bounded tasks:

1. **Audit Jejak Sistem:** inspect a supplied event list and count entries marked `ERROR` using a `for` loop and `if` condition.
2. **Inventaris Rekaman:** count the references supplied by a second source using `len()`.

The missions intentionally teach different concepts and use deterministic, locally evaluated fixtures. The player must not modify source records or infer that the discrepancy is malicious merely because it exists.

The closing report preserves three categories:
- **Observed:** the number of error entries in the supplied log and the number of references in the second source.
- **Not established:** whether the timestamps reflect tampering, clock drift, queue delay, or another cause.
- **Next question:** why the two systems' receipt records differ and what additional evidence would distinguish the possible explanations.

The discrepancy is an unresolved lead, not a solved case. It can support a later investigation only after new evidence and story details are deliberately authored and approved.

The receipt discrepancy and its four-mission learning sequence are approved First Contact canon. Any later case, antagonist, or explanation for the discrepancy requires separate story approval.

## 6.4 Receipt Trace and Source Comparison — Final First Contact Missions

The initial discrepancy remains the same unresolved training case. These four follow-up missions deepen the investigation without introducing a new case, real-world target, or unapproved antagonist.

3. **Penelusuran Referensi:** Freya supplies a small, fictional receipt list containing source labels, record references, and timestamps. The player uses `enumerate()`, `for`, and `if` to locate entries for `RX-14` while preserving each entry's original index. The evaluator checks more than one fixture so a hard-coded output cannot pass.
4. **Perbandingan Dua Sumber:** the player uses list indexing to assign `receipts[0]` to `primary` and `receipts[1]` to `archive`, then prints both records. The evaluator supplies more than one pair to ensure the program reads the actual list rather than printing fixed text.

5. **Normalisasi Waktu:** the two receipt timestamps are also supplied as numeric strings representing seconds since midnight. The player uses `int()`, list indexing, named variables, and subtraction to calculate a signed delta. Multiple fixtures deliberately include equal and reversed source order; a negative delta is valid and must not be mistaken for an error.
6. **Validasi Selisih:** the player uses `abs()` on the difference between the two normalized timestamps. Multiple fixtures verify that the magnitude is non-negative, including when the source order is reversed.

The two additional missions deepen the same receipt discrepancy. They do not create another incident or establish a cause. The final report follows only after both exercises, and it continues to distinguish measured values from interpretation.

The final First Contact report records the two receipt entries as evidence: A.E.G.I.S. records `RX-14` at `09:41:12`, while ARCHIVE records the same reference at `09:41:16`. The four-second difference is observable; its cause is not established.

The player must distinguish:
- **Observed:** two sources contain the same reference with a four-second difference in recorded receipt time.
- **Not established:** whether the difference comes from tampering, queue delay, clock synchronization, or another recording mechanism.
- **Next question:** what additional authorized evidence would distinguish those explanations.

This concludes the First Contact orientation chapter, not the wider recruitment campaign. Freya accepts the report and leaves the discrepancy marked for later review. The author-only fact that Freya initiated the recruitment order remains undisclosed.


# 7. The Primary Mentor

The player has one primary human mentor from the beginning of recruitment.

The mentor is a recurring character—not a tutorial menu, mission dispatcher, or interchangeable instructor.

## 7.1 Role and personality

The primary mentor is **Freya Knox, a highly intelligent, relaxed, playful, and very goofy woman**. Her humor can be so unexpected or absurd that other agents sometimes wonder whether she is taking the situation seriously.

That first impression is deliberately misleading: she is exceptionally capable and understands the work, the evidence, and the risks.

**First-contact behavior (approved):** At first contact, she communicates in a composed, professional manner, establishes the seriousness of the situation, and earns a baseline of credibility. As the player gets to know her, her goofy personality becomes increasingly visible. This does not mean humor is restricted to downtime: once rapport has developed, she often keeps making jokes and playful remarks even while teaching serious or complicated material.

- **Power-inspired personality (approved):** Her characterization takes inspiration from Power in *Chainsaw Man*, without copying the character. She is boastful, excessively confident, mischievous, impulsive in her humor, expressive, and often behaves in a ridiculous or childish way. She may loudly praise her own genius, tease the player, make outrageous claims, or act as if she is unquestionably A.E.G.I.S.'s greatest mentor.
- **Humor is part of her teaching method.** She explains difficult ideas with funny analogies, absurd examples, playful challenges, exaggerated reactions, and unexpected remarks. The goal is to make intimidating concepts easier to understand, reduce the pressure of making mistakes, and encourage the player to keep asking questions.
- She is genuinely sharp: she connects information quickly, notices details others overlook, and understands the risks of an operation. Her boastful manner is comic personality, not a substitute for demonstrated competence.
- She can tease, joke, and behave ridiculously without becoming careless. When a situation is genuinely dangerous or urgent, she can switch immediately into a focused, decisive, professional mode.
- Her teasing must never humiliate the player, make a beginner feel stupid, trivialize victims, dismiss sensitive evidence, or make genuine danger seem harmless.
- The mentor may sometimes behave as though the recruitment decision was an impulsive joke. In reality, selecting the player was a deliberate decision.

### Why this mentor chose the player

At the time of an urgent A.E.G.I.S. situation, the mentor selects an ordinary person who has no formal programming knowledge or intelligence training. To other agents, this seems absurd: why bring an untrained civilian into an operation that needs technical help?

The mentor has already reviewed a dossier of information about the player-character and believes it reveals potential that the player has not yet recognized. The mentor is not choosing the player because they are secretly an expert. The player's value lies in qualities that can be developed—such as curiosity, pattern recognition, persistence, unusual problem-solving, or another trait that the story will establish through evidence.

The exact data source, how the mentor obtained access to it, what first drew their attention, and how much they know remain **unresolved story mysteries**. Reveal these details gradually and make the explanation credible. Do not use omniscient profiling as a shortcut, and do not suggest that the fictional dossier contains or represents the real player's private personal data.

- Welcomes the player into the A.E.G.I.S. process and explains the immediate situation.
- Guides the player's early development without pretending the player is already an expert.
- Provides continuity between cases and remembers relevant player choices and demonstrated skills through game state.
- Helps interpret organizational context, but is not always correct or fully informed.
- Introduces specialists when a task requires expertise outside the mentor's own field.
- Returns to the lead conversation naturally after the specialist handoff.

## 7.2 Dialogue presentation

The default presentation for conversations between the player and characters is an in-game **chat interface**.

- Freya's conversations with the player appear as messages in a secure A.E.G.I.S. messaging interface, not as default visual-novel dialogue boxes.
- Messenger opens to a **contact list**. The player selects Freya, NEXUS, or another available agent to open that contact's conversation; a single shared chat is not the default landing view.
- Contacts become available as characters are introduced and the player's access permits. Returning from a conversation leads back to the contact list; closing Messenger returns to the previous game screen without losing operation context.
- Messages identify their sender and use distinguishable chat bubbles for the character and player.
- When a response is needed, present a set of **predefined authored reply choices** as messages the player can send. The initial version does not accept free-text replies.
- Dialogue choices in the initial version are **cosmetic**. They select the player's immediate wording and may produce a different immediate response, but they do not alter relationship values, unlock content, change mission access, or branch the main story. Do not implement persistent dialogue-choice flags or a relationship meter for this version.
- Preserve conversation history so the player can review important instructions, clues, and relationship moments.
- Use notifications or unread indicators sparingly for new or consequential messages.
- The same general approach can support NEXUS and specialist conversations, while preserving each sender's distinct voice and role.
- The conversation system may later gain optional AI-assisted interaction through an external API such as the OpenAI API. This is a future possibility, not a launch requirement. Core story events, mission progression, relationship state, and consequential decisions must remain controlled by the game and must work without an AI service.
- The exact visual layout and optional messaging features remain open for later UI design.

The opening mentor conversation uses authored, cosmetic responses rather than free-text or relationship branching. Freya's initial replies should reinforce the player's learning process: begin with the current briefing, normalize asking questions, and preserve the unanswered question of why the player was selected. When the player asks why Freya chose them, she must not reveal that she initiated the recruitment order. NEXUS replies should remain system-oriented and factual: report local personnel progress, direct the player to authorized in-game records, and explain that core campaign functions operate offline. These replies may acknowledge current mission progress, but must not invent evidence or unlock access outside the explicit game-state rules.


## 7.3 Relationship portrayal

The mentor-player relationship may become warmer, more familiar, or more strained through authored story events and character development. However, in the initial version:

- Do not implement a numerical relationship or trust meter.
- Cosmetic dialogue selections do not create persistent relationship consequences.
- The main story does not branch based on the player's selected chat replies.
- Relationship changes, when needed, are authored as part of the narrative itself rather than calculated from cosmetic response choices.

A more consequential relationship system may be considered in a later design phase. Romance is not assumed or required.

The mentor's exact age, facial features, hairstyle, clothing, accessories, detailed history, exact expertise, and the full truth behind the player dossier remain open until designed and approved. Her gender, broad personality direction, **youthful and energetic appearance direction**, humor-as-teaching approach, and recruitment rationale above are established story decisions.

# 8. Specialists and Recurring Characters

Specialists should be introduced because the story or operation needs their expertise, not merely to populate a character gallery.

Initial role slots:
- **Primary Mentor:** the player's central human guide.
- **Technical Specialist:** supports software, devices, systems, and embedded/IoT-related operations when those subjects enter the campaign.
- **Intelligence Analyst:** helps assess sources, patterns, uncertainty, and competing explanations.
- **Investigator:** guides evidence handling, timelines, and case reconstruction.
- **Field Operative:** provides context for authorized field assignments and practical constraints.
- **Command Representative:** communicates authorization, strategic priorities, and organizational consequences.

A specialist handoff should be explicit and natural: the mentor explains why another expert is joining, introduces them by role, and lets them lead the relevant subject. The specialist should have a distinct voice and viewpoint. The mentor remains the player's primary relationship.

Do not finalize character names, faces, biographies, or interpersonal subplots until they have been separately designed.

# 9. NEXUS — Narrative Function

NEXUS is the A.E.G.I.S. AI communication-intelligence system.

NEXUS can deliver secure messages, contextual alerts, operational updates, records, and learning support. It can explain concepts and offer graduated hints, but it should not replace human relationships or solve every case.

Narrative distinction:
- The **mentor** provides human judgment, continuity, relationship, and personal guidance.
- **NEXUS** provides system-level communication, retrieval, structured assistance, and contextual operational support.
- **Specialists** provide focused human expertise when the subject requires it.

NEXUS may have a recognizable communication style, but should not be written as a human pretending to be a person.

# 10. Mission Narrative Structure

The initial campaign uses a **hybrid mission system**. Each operation gives the player clear objectives and guided stages, while allowing meaningful interaction with relevant in-game tools. The player should not be forced through a rigid chain of tutorial screens, nor dropped into an unrestricted workspace without direction.

- Present the current objective and enough context to understand the task.
- Let the player use appropriate unlocked tools—such as Terminal, Intelligence Database, Dossier, or Analysis—when they are relevant to the operation.
- Preserve room for the player to inspect evidence, experiment, compare findings, and decide what to submit.
- Use contextual explanations and graduated hints for unfamiliar concepts; do not automatically solve the task.
- Introduce tools and complexity progressively. A mission should only require the tools and knowledge appropriate to its stage in the campaign.
- Preserve mission progress and working context when the player moves between an operation and its applications.

Every case should follow a coherent investigative rhythm:

1. **Inciting event:** a discrepancy, report, request, or unexpected discovery.
2. **Briefing:** what is known, who supplied it, and what remains uncertain.
3. **Dossier:** relevant fictional records, evidence, constraints, and objectives.
4. **Investigation:** compare information, identify gaps, and form hypotheses.
5. **Technical operation:** use an appropriate programming or technical concept to process or inspect the simulated material.
6. **Complication:** new evidence challenges an assumption or creates a decision.
7. **Finding:** the player states what the evidence supports, without claiming more than it proves.
8. **Evaluation and debrief:** explain technical performance and investigative reasoning.
9. **Consequence:** update story flags, access, relationships, or case status where appropriate.
10. **Forward clue:** leave a code/syntax clue that foreshadows the next mission.

Not every mission needs all ten stages as separate screens. The rhythm should guide pacing, not force repetitive UI.

## 10.1 Hybrid Mission Evaluation

Evaluate each operation according to the kind of work the player is asked to do:

- **Technical tasks:** check required behavior, execution results, relevant concept use, and stated constraints. Accept different valid implementations unless the task specifically teaches a required syntax form.
- **Investigative tasks:** check whether conclusions are supported by the case evidence and whether the player distinguishes confirmed facts from inference or uncertainty. Accept different defensible wording and conclusions when they satisfy the evidence criteria.
- **Combined tasks:** assess technical performance and investigative reasoning separately. Correct code does not automatically make an interpretation reliable, and a plausible interpretation does not make a failed technical task successful.
- **Debrief:** explain what the player did well, what remains unsupported or incorrect, and how to improve. Use criteria defined for the operation rather than arbitrary judgment.
- **Authored outcomes:** mission completion and story progression must be determined by explicit, testable criteria authored for the mission. They must not depend on an AI service.

Keep RUN (execute and inspect) separate from SUBMIT (evaluate against mission criteria). A run is not itself a submission or a guarantee of mission completion.

## 10.2 Failure and Retry

The initial campaign uses a **retry-first approach**. A failed submission is a learning opportunity, not an immediate story punishment.

- Explain the unmet requirement and the reason for failure in clear, beginner-appropriate language.
- Allow the player to revise the work and submit again without restarting the whole operation.
- Offer graduated hints: start with a conceptual reminder, then give a more focused clue if needed. Do not reveal the full solution by default.
- Preserve the player's working context and relevant mission progress between attempts.
- In the initial version, failed attempts do not reduce XP or rank, change relationships, lock mission access, or branch the story. Do not introduce retry penalties or consequence-based failure outcomes.
- Mission completion still depends on meeting the explicit criteria authored for that operation. The player may experiment through RUN before submitting again.
- On success, explain what requirements were met and what the player has learned or demonstrated.

This approach encourages beginners to experiment, understand mistakes, and improve without fear of losing campaign progress. Consequence-based failure can be reconsidered separately after the core mission loop is proven.


# 11. Code and Story Clues

Every mission should contain a code- or syntax-based clue that points toward the next mission, as established in `GAME_CONCEPT.md`.

Clues may appear in:
- Terminal output or a system log.
- A line in a fictional source file.
- A field name in a database record.
- A decrypted message.
- A dossier annotation or evidence label.
- A specialist's explanation or a debrief conversation.

A clue should reward curiosity and memory, not require guessing an unexplained answer. The next mission must teach the new concept and provide appropriate hints. In the current First Contact implementation, the clue is shown in the successful mission debrief and points toward the next narrative or technical step.

Track each clue in the content plan: clue source, literal fragment, intended next concept, foreshadowing purpose, and where the connection is resolved.

# 12. Player Choices and Consequences

Choices should be meaningful, understandable in context, and proportionate to the player's knowledge at the time.

Possible consequence dimensions:
- Trust and working relationships.
- Which evidence is prioritized or reviewed.
- Whether a conclusion is marked confirmed, inferred, or unresolved.
- Which authorized specialist is consulted.
- Which optional briefing or conversation becomes available.
- How A.E.G.I.S. records the player's judgment.
- The timing or framing of later revelations.

Avoid fake choices that promise major consequences but always produce the same outcome. Also avoid punishing beginners for not knowing technical information they have not yet learned.

Some choices may change dialogue only; others may alter evidence access or later events. Be clear about which type is being used during implementation.

# 13. Evidence, Truth, and Continuity Rules

- Every important claim should have a source within the fictional world.
- Track evidence as **confirmed**, **inferred**, **disputed**, or **unverified** where useful.
- Distinguish what the player knows, what the mentor knows, what a specialist knows, and what A.E.G.I.S. leadership knows.
- Do not let a character act on information they have not received.
- Maintain a campaign timeline and record important player decisions.
- A later reveal may reinterpret earlier evidence, but should not arbitrarily invalidate established facts.
- Keep technical tasks consistent with the player's demonstrated learning level.
- Preserve the separation between the correctness of code and the reliability of the data being processed.
- Use fictional people, organizations, companies, cases, and datasets for principal plot allegations.

# 14. Tone, Pacing, and Presentation

- Begin personally and mysteriously; widen the scope gradually.
- Balance cinematic scenes with interactive investigation and practical operations.
- Give characters room for warmth, humor, uncertainty, and disagreement. Constant grim seriousness becomes monotonous.
- Use silence, incomplete information, secure messages, environmental details, and restrained interface effects to build tension.
- Do not turn every scene into exposition. Let the player discover information through action.
- Avoid making A.E.G.I.S. universally right or the opposing force cartoonishly evil.
- Do not overuse sudden betrayals. Earn major twists through earlier clues.
- Keep the player central: the player should make observations, ask questions, perform analysis, and decide what to report.

# 15. Safety and Fictional Boundaries

All principal cases, targets, organizations, and allegations are fictional. Real locations may inspire atmosphere, but do not imply wrongdoing by real people or organizations.

Technical operations must remain controlled simulations or explicitly authorized learning environments. The narrative may portray digital investigations, but mission instructions must not become real-world guidance for unauthorized access, credential theft, malware, covert surveillance, or attacks.

# 16. Production Workflow for Story Content

Before implementing a chapter or case, define:

- Campaign and chapter purpose.
- Player's narrative state and what the player knows.
- Character appearances and knowledge boundaries.
- Opening incident and central investigative question.
- Dossier contents and evidence provenance.
- Mission sequence and learning objectives.
- Code/syntax clue to the next mission.
- Player choices and their actual consequences.
- Evaluation and debriefing outcomes.
- New story facts, unresolved questions, and continuity updates.

Keep story content data-driven where practical. Separate dialogue, evidence, mission requirements, and evaluation logic. Do not hardcode the entire campaign into one screen or one large conditional block.

## North Star

**Cyber Protocol is the story of an ordinary person becoming an intelligence operative—not by becoming the strongest fighter, but by learning to see patterns, question assumptions, understand technology, earn trust, and make difficult decisions when the truth is incomplete.**

# 17. Runtime, Persistence, and Connectivity

## 17.1 Local Code Execution

The initial campaign uses a **local Python runtime on the Android device**. Mission code is executed on-device rather than sent to a remote server.

- Python integration follows the project's Kotlin + Jetpack Compose + Chaquopy direction.
- The mission runner must keep player code execution controlled and separate from privileged app operations.
- Apply practical limits for execution time and output, validate inputs, and restrict access to unrelated files, network, shell/process execution, and privileged Android capabilities.
- The game must not imply that using Chaquopy alone automatically makes arbitrary Python code safe. Isolation and restrictions require deliberate implementation and testing.
- RUN displays execution output or errors; SUBMIT separately evaluates mission requirements.

## 17.2 Autosave, Code Drafts, and Local Progress

The initial version uses **autosave for campaign progress**, but player-written mission code is saved at **deliberate save points and mission checkpoints**, not on every keystroke.

- Automatically save campaign progress at meaningful story and gameplay checkpoints, including story beats, mission-stage transitions, successful submissions, debriefs, and progression or unlock changes.
- Give the player a deliberate **Save** action for the current code draft. Also save the code when a mission stage is successfully completed or another clearly defined mission checkpoint is reached.
- Do not write every code-editor change to persistent storage. Clearly communicate whether the latest edits have been saved.
- Preserve saved code drafts with their campaign, mission, and stage context so the player can resume the correct work.
- After interruption or app restart, restore the latest valid campaign checkpoint and the most recently saved code draft.
- Avoid unnecessary writes for every small interaction; temporary presentation state does not need to persist by default.

## 17.3 Offline-first Campaign

The initial campaign must remain playable without an internet connection.

- Authored story, fixed-choice dialogue, mission content, learning material, local code execution, evaluation, progression, and autosave function on-device.
- No server, external API, live AI model, account, or API key is required for core campaign progression or mission completion.
- Future online features, such as optional AI-assisted conversation or cloud synchronization, are supplemental and must degrade gracefully when offline.
- Any future online feature must not break the core offline campaign.
